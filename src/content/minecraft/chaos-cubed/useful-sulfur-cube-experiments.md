**The most useful Sulfur Cube experiment is a controlled test lane: keep the distance and starting position the same, change one absorbed block, then compare speed, bounce and stopping distance.** It turns random-looking chaos into something a beginner can measure.

## Build a simple test area

Use a flat strip about 20 blocks long with walls on both sides and a soft backstop. Mark every two blocks. Keep shears, an empty bucket and replacement blocks in a chest outside the lane. Test one cube at a time.

## Ten experiments to try

1. **Wood versus stone bounce:** wood should produce fast, high bounces; stone-like blocks are slower but still highly bouncy.
2. **Ice stopping distance:** compare an icy block with metal. Ice removes bounce and lowers ground friction, so it should slide much farther.
3. **Wool in water:** wool creates a light, buoyant cube with strong air drag. Compare it with a loose-soil block, which is also buoyant but behaves differently.
4. **Honeycomb sticky finish:** put a honeycomb-absorbed cube at the end of a short slope and compare its stopping behaviour with an organic block.
5. **Soul sand resistance:** apply the same push to a soul-sand cube and a wooden cube. Mark the distance travelled.
6. **Safe magma boundary test:** let a magma-loaded cube touch a non-player mob only in a controlled Creative test world, or observe it from behind glass. It damages entities on contact.
7. **Minecart transport:** Sulfur Cubes cannot enter boats in Java 26.2, but they can ride minecarts. Test a simple station for moving one around your base.
8. **Dispenser block swap:** point a dispenser at a cube and test inserting, swapping and shearing absorbed blocks without entering the lane.
9. **Geyser launch:** place a non-explosive cube over a properly built geyser and measure how the random magma eruption moves it.
10. **Sculk eruption alarm:** connect a Sculk Sensor to a lamp near a magma geyser. Java 26.2 says eruptions emit game events at their start and end.

## Keep the results fair

Change only one variable at a time. Use the same cube size, floor, push direction and starting marker. Write the block, distance and any surprising movement on signs or in a book. That is a genuine in-world observation, not a guess from how a block looks.
